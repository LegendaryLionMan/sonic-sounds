## Concept

Pyro Altar front person Cole Sterling looks back at his late-teen / early-20s years (roughly age 15 to 22) with adult eyes. The album is a loose collection of memory vignettes — each track a standalone fragment of those years, not a single chronological arc. The central emotional thread is the slow-realization question **"where did the time go?"** — tenderness, not bitterness; looking back at youth with adult eyes, sometimes amused, sometimes wistful, but never cruel.

The album is autobiographical in texture but fictional in detail. Scenes are drawn from the feeling of those years, not documentary re-tellings. Held together by the Pyro Altar sonic identity (90s grunge + FF melodic) and Cole's Velvet Revolver-style vocal — the same voice looking back at the same memories from the same band.

**Three sub-decisions on the way to this concept:**
- M01.1 = **late teens / coming-of-age (15–22)** — the era where the band form, the music takes over, the first shows, the first heartbreak, the "don't know who I am yet" phase
- M01.2 = **"where did the time go?"** — the slow realization that youth is over, looking back with tenderness
- M01.3 = **loose vignettes, no single arc** — each track a standalone memory fragment

**Reference anchors** (M04):
- **Foo Fighters — In Your Honor** — the canonical nostalgia record. Acoustic-side tracks 'Razor' and 'Cold Day in the Sun' are the reference for tenderness-with-edge
- **Nirvana — In Utero** — the raw teenage-voice counterweight. 'Pennyroyal Tea' and 'All Apologies' for unrestrained angst

## Motif (the album's spine)

**Recurring 4-chord progression (G–C–D–Em)** — the most basic open-G 4-chord shape, the kind a 15-year-old learns first. Played differently each time it appears:

- Track 1 (Razor): clean electric arpeggio — the first guitar memory
- Track 5 (Twenty-Two): full-band power chords — the realization
- Track 9 (Freeway): dirty 12-string — the van, the highway
- Track 11 (Junior): solo acoustic — addressing the younger self
- Track 12 (Standing Still): full-band with strings — the resolution

The progression is the album's spine — recognizable as the same emotional core, but aged across the tracks. The listener doesn't need to notice this consciously; the album just feels like one continuous look-back.
